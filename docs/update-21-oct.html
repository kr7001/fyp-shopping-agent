The big picture

A shopping agent answers from the page it can see

  • When a price, size or stock level only appears after a click, which this project calls interaction-gated information, shopping agents often answer anyway, wrongly and with no sign of doubt.
  • The project traced this to its cause in a real open-source agent, which trusts the four product cards on screen and stops looking.
  • It built a block that sits in front of any agent. The block plans what to open and when to stop, asks the user when a question is ambiguous, and checks the answer.
  • It tested the block on a new clothing market it had never seen, with the test plan written down before the market existed.
  • The block was right on 12 of 12 runs where the shipped agent was right on 4. It opened a median of 6 listings instead of 15, at less than half the cost of opening every listing. All three tests planned in advance passed, and they passed again on a second model.

What "3 of 3 tests passed" means

Before the run, the plan named three comparisons and the test to use for each. Fixing them in advance stops anyone from choosing, after seeing the data, the comparisons that happen to look good.

  • H1. The block is right more often than the shipped agent. 12 of 12 runs against 4 of 12, p = 0.001.
  • H2. The block opens fewer listings than "open everything". A median of 6 against 15, p = 0.00025.
  • H3. The block costs less a run than "open everything". A median of $0.20 against $0.41, p = 0.000016.

A p-value says how often a difference this large would appear by chance if the block were in fact no better. p = 0.001 means about 1 time in 1,000. Three tests give three chances to be fooled, so Holm's correction raises each p-value to allow for that. After it the three are 0.001, 0.0005 and 0.00005, all far below the usual cut-off of 0.05.

The problem in one example

The test shop is a self-hosted copy of the WebArena e-commerce site, which runs on Magento, with a market this project generated. The market holds 15 listings of the same tee from 15 sellers. The results page shows one price per listing, the cheapest size that can be bought. A size's own price and stock appear only after choosing that size inside the listing. So the question "which seller is cheapest for size M?" cannot be answered from the results page.

On the held-out market, Ashcombe, the cheapest-looking card (Quarles, $18.11) sits in the first row. The answer is Copperfield Outfitters at $23.43, shown 11th. Six listings must be opened to be sure.

The real-world motivation was observed by hand in August 2026, with no automation. A live marketplace listing advertised four pack sizes but showed one price at a time. A live AI shopping assistant asked for the cheapest 9.5 L carton named a seller as 27% cheaper and was wrong, with no sign of doubt. The page's structured data published a single price rather than the range, so the information was missing rather than contradicted.

  1. One new market. Ashcombe is one draw from the generator. A second new market would show the result is not luck of the draw.

The ask-back was not applied under one reading. On "how many listings offer size M" the block asked which reading was meant and was told "listed in the menu, sold out or not". The block

A step that runs before the shopping agent

The block is not an agent and does not replace one. It hands text to whichever agent is used, so it is agent-agnostic. It makes three moves.

  1. Plan

    Read the question and the results page. Give the agent the listing count, the listings sorted by advertised price with their URLs, and a strategy with a stop rule. Open these, in this order, and stop when no unopened listing can change the answer.

  2. Ask, push back or pass through

    Ask when the question has two readings. Refuse, for $0, when the question needs something the shop does not show, such as delivery time or "softest". Pass a non-shopping question straight to the agent and count it.

  3. Check

    After the agent answers, decide from the page and the agent's own log whether the answer is supported by what was opened. Refuse it if not. The check never reads an answer key.

How it works, in eight steps

1. Results page

The results page is fetched over HTTP and parsed into sellers, advertised prices and URLs. Nothing is opened yet.

Why it is partly AI and partly rules

A model can parse the question, which takes judgement. Rules do the rest, because stop rules can be proved, checks can refuse an answer the model is sure about, and the same input gives the same text every time. A fully agentic block would be "open everything" with extra steps, at its cost.

The stop rule for "cheapest" is branch and bound. A listing's advertised price is the cheapest size you can buy in it, so a listing advertised above the best price already found cannot hold a cheaper size and is skipped. The assumption, checked live on both markets, is that the card advertises no more than the cheapest buyable size. In a real shop it can break, through a stale price index, a default variant on the card, or member prices.

When it asks or pushes back

  • Two readings of a verb like offer, carry, stock, sell or have about a size, with nothing in the question that settles it. The block asks which reading.
  • A vague price word with no number, such as "a cheap tee". The block asks for the threshold.
  • An attribute the shop does not show. The block says so and runs nothing.
  • When one reading has no answer on the shop, for example the price of a sold-out size is not shown, the block takes the other reading itself and says so.

Decisions and why

The choices that shaped the result

Ten decisions carry most of the weight. Open each for its reason. The rest are in the collapsible below, and the full dated list is in the repository at docs/decisions-20-oct.html.

Answering, not buyingscope

Every purchase starts with a question. Which offer, which price, which size, is it in stock. A wrong answer becomes a wrong purchase one click later. Buying, discounts, coupons and delivery are out of scope and are stated as limits.

The block is agent-agnosticdesign

It hands text to any agent and changes none of the agent's code. Test 3 showed the errors it targets appear in a second agent too, so the fix should not live inside one agent.

A model parses, rules plan and checkdesign

Parsing takes judgement, so a model may do it. Stop rules can be proved, checks can refuse an answer the model is sure about, and the same input gives the same text every time, so rules do everything after the parse.

Ask back on a question with two readings rather than guessdesign

From the "offer" finding on 8 October. Every agent that explained itself read the ambiguous count question the other way from the key. Nothing more could be opened, so the only fix is to ask.

Refuse for $0 anything the shop does not showdesign

Whatever the word. A question about the "softest" tee once cost $0.80 in a fallback that opened all 15 listings and could not answer. Now the block says the attribute is not shown and runs nothing.

The run plan was committed before the held-out market existedfairness

The protocol was committed at noon on 20 October and the market generated afterwards by a fixed rule, the first seed from 204 that passes the generator's checks, with the brand picked by a fixed rule too. Nothing was chosen by looking at it.

A fresh browser profile for every runfairness

The audit found every run since 8 September had shared one profile, with a shop session lasting a year. One stray click in one run could have shown in every later run. All four arms now get the same fresh profile.

A fourth arm, the block without its stop rulefairness

On Thornwood, 41% of the browser actions the block saved came from handing the agent the list of listings rather than from deciding when to stop. Without this arm an examiner could say the gain is just the list. It runs on the three questions where the stop rule acts.

The headline correctness measure leaves out the "offer" count questionfairness

On that question whoever matches the key's reading is right, and only the block is told the reading. So it is reported separately under both readings, and the main score uses the four questions whose answer does not depend on the reading.

Three tests fixed in advance, with a correction for testing three things, and no equivalence claimsfairness

Block against shipped on correctness, block against open everything on listings opened and on cost, Holm-corrected. Twelve runs per arm cannot prove two arms are equal, so "as accurate as open everything" is reported as counts with an interval, never as a test result.

See every other decision
  • Reviews in scope but later. They gate by pages of reviews rather than by a click, and need their own page reader, check and keys.
  • Five sentences in the block's instructions corrected because they were not always true of the shop, for example "a sold-out size cannot be chosen", which a script can force. Each was reworded only as far as makes it true. This version is the one frozen for the claim, block v12.
  • Rotated arm order across three rounds, so any drift in the model service falls on every arm alike, as one command so the guard asks once.
  • Broken runs defined from the log before any answer is read. A silence over 60 seconds or a provider error is re-run once. A run that ran out of steps counts as wrong and is reported separately.
  • The shop's price leak left in place and counted. The theme reveals a sold-out size's price if a script forces the menu. It is open to every arm alike, real shops that render prices in the browser can behave the same way, and changing the shop hours before the run would have undone its 20 live checks.
  • Thornwood kept untouched, so earlier results stay reproducible. The claim market uses its own product codes and addresses.
  • Kelsall taken off the brand list before any seed. It is a seller's first name in the generator, and the scorer matches a seller by its first word, so every answer naming the brand would have scored as naming that seller.
  • The page rule's code corrected before any seed. It had read a listing's price as its cheapest size including sold-out ones, where the card shows the cheapest size you can buy. On this market the correction changed nothing.
  • "Under $24" means strictly under, everywhere. The block had read it as "at most" while the keys read it as "less than". It affects only the newer question shapes, but the two must agree.
  • The audit's fixes landed after the market was generated but before any run on it. The protocol's clause was reworded, with reasons, before any data, recorded at 14:08. Every fix came from Thornwood or the code, and no run on Ashcombe existed, so the claim market's results could not have shaped the block.
  • The newer question shapes are not in the claim run. They had one development run each and the rule-based parser is weakest on them. They ran after the claim as their own batch.
  • The reworded-question check counts 24 of 30. Six rewordings changed the question itself, so no correct parse can match the original. That rule was written into the file before any paid call.
  • Nothing committed between the verifier's stamp and the run. The guard only runs a paid command at exactly the stamped commit.

The full list with timestamps is docs/decisions-20-oct.html in the repository, and the dated decision log is in README.md.

How the work is checked

Nothing is spent, and no figure is published, without a check

  • Pre-registration. The claim protocol fixed the arms, the measures, the tests and the exclusions before the market existed. Every later change is dated with its reason.
  • An independent verifier. An AI module that never writes code checks every code change and every result against the raw files, and stamps a pass before any paid run. On 20 October alone it failed plans or wording four times before passing them, each time with a real fault.
  • A spend guard. No paid run without a declared cost, the verifier's stamp at the exact commit, and the student's click in a dialog.
  • Figures from commands. Every figure comes from a command over the raw run files. The site and the analysis are regenerated, never typed.
  • Unit tests. 417 tests, each checked to fail when the fault it guards against is planted.
What the audit and the verifier caught before money was spent
  • A seller's name, Kelsall, on the brand list. It would have scored every answer as naming that seller.
  • A shared browser session across all runs since 8 September.
  • Five false sentences in the agent's instructions, among them "a sold-out size cannot be chosen".
  • A size-menu reader that took a partial view for the whole menu, twice, in two different ways.
  • A page rule reading the wrong price, the cheapest size including sold-out ones, where the card shows the cheapest buyable size.
  • Three wrong figures in the 7 October update, found when every figure was re-derived from the raw files.

What this adds up to

Five contributions, and what each dollar bought

This is the section to know by heart. Each contribution below is a thing the report can claim, with the evidence on this page behind it.

RQ2 · the failure

A measured, explained failure

A production open-source shopping agent stops at the four product cards in its viewport and answers confidently and wrongly. On Thornwood it opened what the answer needed in 3 of 15 runs, and on the held-out market it was correct in 4 of 12. The cause is one default in its card reader. Switching it off takes opened-what-it-needs to 15 of 15. A second agent built from scratch shows the remaining errors, ambiguity and reading a price too early, are not specific to one agent.

RQ3 · the method

A method that fixes it at low cost

The block reaches the correctness of exhaustive exploration while opening fewer than half the listings at less than half the cost, on a held-out market under pre-registered tests. The same three tests held again on a second model, DeepSeek V4 Flash. About half of the saving comes from the stop rule itself, with the rest from handing the agent the list and the plan.

Finding

Clarification only helps if the agent applies the answer

Asking the user back resolves an ambiguous question only if the agent then applies the answer. On the held-out market Haiku did not apply it under one reading, in all three runs, while DeepSeek did on exactly the same text. This is a new, recorded kind of error, and it is a result in its own right.

Environment

A shop where gating exists

The WebArena shop had no products whose variants have different prices. All 104,368 products are simple products. This project adds generated markets with gated per-variant prices and stock, answer keys computed from the data, and a live checker. It is a candidate contribution to WebArena-Verified, claimed only if a pull request is merged.

Way of working

Pre-registration, a verifier and a spend guard

Together they caught several result-breaking flaws before any money was spent. Without them the claim run would have run with a shared browser session across arms, false instructions to the agent, and a brand that collides with a seller name. Any of those could have been used to dismiss the result.
