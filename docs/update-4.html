Shopping agents and hidden information

What was done since 7 October, in plain terms. Every number here was checked against the raw run files by an independent verifier.

1. The problem, in one example

Our test shop has 15 listings of the same "Thornwood tee" from 15 sellers. The search results page shows one price per listing: its cheapest size. The price and stock of size M only appear after you click M inside the listing.

So if you ask "which seller is cheapest for size M?", the results page cannot tell you. The agent has to open listings. The cheapest-looking card (Rookwood, $11.87) does not even sell M. The real answer is Alderney at $25.77, and 8 listings have to be opened to be sure.

A shopping agent that answers from the results page alone is confidently wrong. That is the failure this project measures and fixes.

2. The five questions every test asks

  1. Lowest price for size M among listings that can actually sell it. Key: $25.77.
  2. Which seller is cheapest for size M, and at what price. Key: Alderney at $25.77.
  3. Does every listing offer size M? Key: No.
  4. How many listings offer size M? Key: 10 (M is in their size menu, even if sold out).
  5. How many listings have M in stock and buyable right now? Key: 5.

Each test runs every question 3 times, so 15 runs a test. Model: Claude Haiku 4.5 via OpenRouter, the same for every test.

4. What the results tell us

Test 1 → 2: the shipped agent stops too early, and we know why. As shipped it opened enough listings in only 3 of 15 runs. It reads the 4 product cards visible in the browser window and trusts them. Switching that one default off took it to 15 of 15 and correct answers from 4 to 11.

Test 3: it is not only an openJiuwen problem. A second agent we wrote from scratch, with no card reader, opened enough listings most of the time and was the cheapest. But it still got answers wrong for two reasons that have nothing to do with the agent's code: it misread the ambiguous word "offer" (next section), and it read a price before the page had updated after clicking a size. Those come from the model and the page. Any agent will have them.

Test 4: "just open everything" is the ceiling, and it is not good enough. It costs 3 times the shipped agent and still gets 4 of 15 wrong, 3 of them on the same ambiguous question.

Test 5: the block was as accurate as opening everything, at half the cost. 13 of 15 correct, against 4 for the shipped agent and 11 for "open everything". It opened a median of 8 listings where "open everything" opens 15, and cost $0.26 a run against $0.52. Its two wrong answers were the model misreading a page (once reporting a card's price as the size's price, once never reading the size menu), not the block stopping too early.

5. The question every test got wrong, and what it taught us

"How many listings offer size M?" Of the 15 listings, 10 have M in their size menu, but only 5 can sell it right now (the other 5 are sold out). The answer key, written before any run, says 10. Every agent in every test answered 5, even the one that opened all 15 listings.

So the agents did not fail to look. They read "offer" as "can sell it now" where the key meant "listed in the menu". The question is ambiguous, and no amount of browsing fixes that.

This is what inspired the block's "ask back" step: when a question has two reasonable readings, ask the user which one they mean before running anything, instead of guessing. We kept the ambiguous questions on purpose, so the block can be measured on catching them.

It worked. In Test 5 the block asked on both "offer" questions and on none of the others. Once told the reading, the agent was right 3 of 3 times under "listed in the menu" and 6 of 6 under "can sell it now".

6. The block

A step that runs before the shopping agent. It is not an agent and does not replace one. It works with whichever agent is used.

  1. Plan. It reads the question and the results page, then hands the agent a plan: here are the 15 listings sorted by advertised price, open them in this order, and stop once no unopened listing can beat the best found. For "cheapest size M" that means 8 listings instead of 15.
  2. Ask back, or push back. If the question has two readings ("offer"), it asks which. If the question needs something the shop does not show (delivery time), it says so and runs nothing. If the question is not about shopping at all, it passes it straight to the agent and counts that this happened.
  3. Check. After the agent answers, it checks from the page alone whether the answer is supported by what was opened, and refuses it if not. It never sees the answer key.

Is the block written by an AI, or fixed rules?

Both, in a deliberate split. A model may parse the question into its logical form (what kind of answer, which attribute, which conditions), because that takes judgement. Fixed rules do everything after: build the plan, decide when to stop, check the answer. Rules can be proved and tested, give the same text every time, and can refuse an answer the model is confident about. We tested the model parser on all 37 questions in the project: it agreed with the rules on the fields that decide the plan 33 times out of 37, for $0.07 in total.

Version 2, and the new question shapes

Version 1 had four fixed strategies. Version 2 handles every question shape the shopping benchmarks contain (cheapest, cheapest with conditions, is there any, the k cheapest, does every, how many, compare two named sellers, reviews), composed from seven building blocks. In one run each on the new shapes: "is there any under $27" and "under $25", "which of these two sellers is cheaper" and "cheapest in XL" were right, "the three cheapest" was wrong. A delivery-time question was refused for $0 because the shop does not show delivery times. A "cheap tee" question with no price was asked back for $0. A poem and a capital-city question went straight to the agent, which was blocked every time it tried to leave the shop.

What the checks found, and what was fixed

  • The block's own answer check was refusing correct "No" answers that did not name a listing. It now accepts a "No" when the opened page shows the size is missing.
  • The verifier caught the block twice reading a partial view of a size menu as the whole menu. Both are fixed, and every menu reading in every stored run was checked against the shop's data: 180 readings, none wrong.
  • An independent audit before the next paid run found nine things that would have weakened the result. All nine are fixed. The main ones: every run since 8 September had shared one browser profile, one sentence in the block's instructions was false for sold-out sizes, and the comparison needed a fourth arm to separate the block's stop rule from simply handing the agent the list of listings.

Research that shaped it

  • Shopping benchmarks (WebShop, WebMall, DeepShop): gave the list of question shapes, and showed agents fail most on vague requirements and filters.
  • Clarifying-question work (DiscoBench, SAGE-Agent): ask only when the ambiguity changes the answer, and score both over-asking and under-asking.
  • Adaptive retrieval (AB-RAG, Stop-RAG): the closest existing work. It decides when to stop from the model's own confidence, so it cannot catch a confident wrong answer. Our stop rule comes from the page (the advertised price as a lower bound), which is the gap we fill.
  • "Model proposes, rules verify" (LLM-Modulo): the pattern the block follows.

7. Scope, and why it matters

  • Answering, not buying. Every purchase an agent makes starts with a question: which offer, what price, which size, in stock? A wrong answer there becomes a wrong purchase one click later. Buying, discounts, coupons and delivery are out of scope and stated as limits.
  • Reviews are in scope and come next. "Do buyers say it leaks?" is the same kind of hidden information, hidden by pages of reviews instead of a click.
  • Agent agnostic. The block hands text to whichever agent is used. Test 3 shows the errors it targets appear in a second agent too.

8. The test on a new market (run on the night of 20 October)

Why a new market. The block was designed and fixed on Thornwood, so Test 5 cannot prove it works in general. The fair test is a market it has never seen.

How it was made fair. The plan (which arms, which questions, which tests, what counts as a broken run) was written and committed before the new market existed. The market was then generated by a fixed rule: the first seed from 204 upward that passes the generator's checks. A new brand, Ashcombe, was picked by a fixed rule too. Nothing was chosen by looking at it.

The new market. 15 Ashcombe tee listings. The answer to "cheapest size M" is Copperfield Outfitters at $23.43, shown 11th on the page. The cheapest-looking card, Quarles at $18.11, sits in the first row. Six listings have to be opened to be sure.

The four arms, each 3 rounds with the order rotated: the shipped agent, "open everything", the block, and the block without its stop rule. That last arm shows how much of the saving comes from deciding when to stop, rather than from handing the agent the list of listings.
